<p class="project-lede">Machine learning increasingly appears in the metabolic modeling of Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell culture, from hybrid models that learn rates inside material balances to Raman soft sensors that infer metabolite concentrations. Because each new clone, scale or product brings few runs, much of this work aims to transfer knowledge from earlier systems, which is the goal of transfer learning. However, the evidence behind a claim of transfer ranges from reconstructing a run from its own measurements, to forecasting after a stated day, to genuine prediction with fixed parameters. In this review, I survey how machine learning and transfer learning have been applied to CHO metabolic models, to the measurements that feed them and to the design of the media they run on. I keep these kinds of evidence apart throughout.</p>

This review follows my [review of CHO cell models and CFD coupling](/projects/cho-cell-model-cfd/) and my [NISTCHO model](/projects/nistcho-cell-culture-model/). Both raised the question of how far a model calibrated on one system can be trusted in another, which is the question that transfer learning addresses. I restrict the review mainly to CHO cells and to work from 2010 onward. Where they illustrate a transfer strategy, I also include simulation studies and a few studies of other cell lines or organisms. The review covers hybrid and machine-learning models, together with the transfer of the sensor calibrations that increasingly supply process data. Media and feed studies show how learned models guide prospective experiments. I include mechanistic models as a benchmark for transfer by refitting, filtering and frozen prediction.

Several reviews cover neighboring ground. They address machine learning for small data in upstream bioprocessing <a href="#ref-1">[1]</a>, transfer learning in bioprocess engineering <a href="#ref-2">[2]</a> and hybrid modeling of biopharmaceutical processes <a href="#ref-3">[3]</a>, <a href="#ref-4">[4]</a>. Others cover machine learning with genome-scale models <a href="#ref-5">[5]</a> and CHO cell modeling as a whole <a href="#ref-6">[6]</a>. Here I focus instead on the evidence that a model or a calibration transfers.

## Three kinds of evidence

Studies of model transfer report three kinds of evidence, and the difference decides what a transferred model can be trusted to do. In a filtered reconstruction, the run's own measurements correct the model's states or parameters as they arrive. In a forecast after a stated cutoff, the model is corrected up to a given day and then runs forward without further data. In a frozen prediction from inoculation, the parameters are fixed before the run begins, and the model runs forward from the initial conditions and process inputs alone. A frozen prediction tests whether the model can carry a new culture forward from those inputs alone. This is the setting of interest for process design and CFD coupling.

<div class="xdr-experiment-plan xdr-data-table" role="region" aria-label="Three kinds of evidence for model transfer and what corrects the model in each" tabindex="0">

| Kind of evidence | What corrects the model during the run | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filtered reconstruction | Each measurement of the run, as it arrives | Dual ensemble Kalman filter of Yu et al. <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a> |
| Forecast after a stated cutoff | Measurements up to a stated day | Forecasts from days 7, 9 and 12 in the same study <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>, and titer forecasts from day 9 for held-out cultures <a href="#ref-8">[8]</a> |
| Frozen prediction from inoculation | Nothing after the initial conditions and process inputs | Fed-batch parameters used to predict perfusion <a href="#ref-9">[9]</a> |

</div>

Two common designs sit between these categories. Xing et al. built their model as a one-day map. Each day's simulation starts from that day's measured concentrations, adjusted for the feed just added, and predicts the next day's pre-feed sample <a href="#ref-10">[10]</a>. In Bayer et al., a hybrid model kept its trained parameters fixed but took each test run's measured glucose and amino acid concentrations as inputs <a href="#ref-11">[11]</a>. Both designs test useful properties, but neither shows that the model can carry a culture forward unaided. I also note whether each result rests on measured CHO data, on simulation or on another organism, because several transfer results discussed below rest on the latter two.

## Model families

Six model families recur in the CHO literature. They differ mainly in the data they require and in how much of their structure is learned rather than prescribed. They also differ in how expensive they are to evaluate when run many times, as in optimization or a coupled CFD simulation.

<div class="xdr-experiment-plan xdr-data-table" role="region" aria-label="CHO model families, the data each requires and its cost per evaluation" tabindex="0">

| Family | Data required | Cost per evaluation | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unstructured kinetic | Daily extracellular metabolites, cell counts and titer | Low | Xing et al. <a href="#ref-10">[10]</a>, Kotidis et al. <a href="#ref-12">[12]</a>, Sietaram et al. <a href="#ref-13">[13]</a> |
| Structured kinetic or kinetic–stoichiometric (dynamic MFA or FBA) | Amino acid or intracellular metabolite profiles and a curated reaction network, sometimes with ¹³C labeling | Medium, with a flux calculation at each step | Robitaille et al. <a href="#ref-14">[14]</a>, Khare et al. <a href="#ref-15">[15]</a>, Reddy et al. <a href="#ref-16">[16]</a> |
| Flux or genome-scale model combined with machine learning | Exchange rates, and omics data for line-specific versions | High, with an optimization at each step | Schinn et al. <a href="#ref-17">[17]</a>, Ramos et al. <a href="#ref-18">[18]</a>, Gopalakrishnan et al. <a href="#ref-19">[19]</a>, Richelle et al. <a href="#ref-20">[20]</a> |
| Hybrid, with learned rates inside material balances | Tens of runs, such as 24 in Pinto et al. and 33 in Bayer et al. | Low to medium, although learned rates may not hold outside the training conditions | Narayanan et al. <a href="#ref-21">[21]</a>, Bayer et al. <a href="#ref-11">[11]</a>, <a href="#ref-22">[22]</a>, Pinto et al. <a href="#ref-23">[23]</a>, Doyle et al. <a href="#ref-24">[24]</a> |
| Physics-informed network or neural ODE | A mechanistic starting model and process data | Low once trained, at research stage | Cui et al. <a href="#ref-25">[25]</a>, Yang et al. <a href="#ref-26">[26]</a> |
| Data-driven sequence model | Tens to hundreds of historical runs | Low once trained | Smiatek et al. <a href="#ref-27">[27]</a>, Park et al. <a href="#ref-28">[28]</a>, Lu et al. <a href="#ref-29">[29]</a> |

</div>

Compact kinetic models are the cheapest family to evaluate, and they need only the extracellular measurements that most fed-batch campaigns already collect. Their weakness is the one this review keeps returning to. Their rate laws are flexible enough to fit almost any single run, so a good fit says little about whether the same parameters will hold elsewhere.

Hybrid models shift the balance toward data. In an early quantitative comparison, Narayanan et al. used a 3.5 L fed-batch dataset. Their hybrid models predicted the time evolution of process variables from the initial and process conditions better than statistical models <a href="#ref-21">[21]</a>. The same group later built a family of models with an increasing share of encoded process knowledge. Purely data-driven models performed poorly with few data and extrapolated poorly, whereas a fully mechanistic model lost accuracy because the added knowledge biased it <a href="#ref-30">[30]</a>. Bayer et al. compared the two approaches on 33 experiments from a four-dimensional fractional factorial design. The hybrid model was more accurate than the mechanistic model on every data partition, but it was also more dependent on which partition it was trained on. Interestingly, a well-chosen set of four experiments already represented the full design space for either model type <a href="#ref-22">[22]</a>. In addition, Doyle et al. compared a Gaussian-process hybrid with a Monod model on two intensified fed-batch processes, using data from development and manufacturing scale. They noted that transient effects of bolus feeding remained a challenge <a href="#ref-24">[24]</a>. For perfusion, Agarwal et al. calibrated a hybrid model of growth and antibody production on three ambr 250 runs, tested it on five more and used it to schedule media exchange <a href="#ref-31">[31]</a>.

Machine learning has also been combined with flux and genome-scale models. Schinn et al. joined a CHO genome-scale model with machine learning to predict the time course of individual amino acid concentrations. Their motivation was that the metabolic model alone underestimates amino acid consumption and forecasts only a short range <a href="#ref-17">[17]</a>. Ramos et al. added empirical constraints to the flux balance analysis of a reduced CHO-K1 network. They derived the constraints by principal component analysis of 27 exchange fluxes measured in 21 reactor experiments, and the hybrid formulation improved the predicted specific growth rate <a href="#ref-18">[18]</a>. Gopalakrishnan et al. used machine learning to split measured uptake and secretion rates between growing and antibody-producing cell states before passing them to a genome-scale model. Their framework predicted final cell density and titer to within 10% of the measured values <a href="#ref-19">[19]</a>. In addition, Pennington et al. linked a hybrid model of a CHO batch culture to enzyme-constrained dynamic flux analysis. The hybrid model reduced the error of the macroscale dynamics to 8.0%, which was 70% below that of the purely mechanistic model <a href="#ref-32">[32]</a>.

Neural networks have also been attached to models of product quality. Kotidis and Kontoravdi applied a neural network to site-specific glycosylation of four CHO-produced glycoproteins. The standalone network gave an average absolute error of 1.1% for a quadruple-knockout fusion protein. The hybrid kinetic model gave about 1.25% on a held-out antibody feeding strategy <a href="#ref-33">[33]</a>. Antonakoudis et al. fed fluxes from a stoichiometric model to a neural network that predicts antibody glycan distributions. They trained it on two feeds and tested it on a third, with fluxes calculated for that culture <a href="#ref-34">[34]</a>.

Physics-informed networks and sequence models sit at the data-driven end. Yang et al. built a physics-informed network for a large-scale pilot CHO fed-batch, in which knowledge from small-scale studies constrains a network trained on the large-scale data. Notably, daily calibration with offline measurements further improved its predictions <a href="#ref-26">[26]</a>. Smiatek et al. trained recurrent networks on 118 runs of a 12,000 L process and on 90 development-scale runs of four antibodies <a href="#ref-27">[27]</a>. Lu et al. extended recurrent networks with the history of the seed train to forecast behavior at production scale <a href="#ref-29">[29]</a>.

Frozen-parameter prediction of new conditions has therefore been shown for changes in feeding strategy <a href="#ref-12">[12]</a>, initial nutrient concentrations <a href="#ref-15">[15]</a> and culture mode <a href="#ref-9">[9]</a>, <a href="#ref-16">[16]</a>, mostly at a fixed scale. Most learned models in the table were instead tested on held-out runs of the process they were trained on <a href="#ref-23">[23]</a>, <a href="#ref-27">[27]</a>, <a href="#ref-20">[20]</a>, <a href="#ref-35">[35]</a>. Hybrid models have also predicted new conditions at the same scale. Nemoto et al. fitted a hybrid model to 29 cultivation conditions of a CHO-MK cell line in 250 mL bioreactors and confirmed its predictions under 15 further conditions <a href="#ref-36">[36]</a>. Richelle et al. transferred growth, death and lysis parameters from fed-batch to perfusion at the same 200 mL working volume <a href="#ref-9">[9]</a>. A hybrid perfusion model illustrates the difficulty of such a step. With the parameters identified in ambr 250 runs, the model of Agarwal et al. deviated from the measured cell density and viability of two 5 L runs after day 12.5. The authors then added terms for the retention of lysed cells and biomaterial by the cell retention device and re-estimated four parameters for each run <a href="#ref-31">[31]</a>. The 1:50 scale result of Bayer et al. needs a closer reading. Their hybrid model kept fixed parameters, but its training data included three 15 L center-point runs, and it estimated each 15 L test run from that run's own measured temperature, glucose and amino acids <a href="#ref-11">[11]</a>. The authors note that, with only offline analytes available from the shake flasks, the model describes process behavior but cannot be used for process control <a href="#ref-11">[11]</a>.

Data-driven models are often tested as forecasts. Park et al. compared algorithms, input features and multistep-ahead strategies for forecasting viable cell density, titer, glucose, lactate and ammonia in CHO fed-batch cultures <a href="#ref-28">[28]</a>. A hybrid Luedeking–Piret regression used data up to day 9 of seven CHO cultures to forecast titer through day 16, with a mean error of 5.85% on day 16 in leave-two-out cross-validation <a href="#ref-8">[8]</a>. At manufacturing scale, Le et al. predicted the final titer and lactate concentration of 243 production runs from early process data <a href="#ref-37">[37]</a>. In addition, Schmitt et al. trained a classifier on 128 fed-batch runs of five clones to forecast whether a run would end in lactate consumption or accumulation. In a prospective test on one clone, a predictive controller steered cultures toward lactate consumption <a href="#ref-38">[38]</a>.

Xing et al. remain a rare large-scale benchmark with published equations and parameter values, and others have since adopted its equations <a href="#ref-10">[10]</a>, <a href="#ref-39">[39]</a>. Their model of 5,000 L fed-batch cultures includes lactate and ammonia in its growth and death rates only above threshold concentrations, and removes ammonia through an explicit term <a href="#ref-10">[10]</a>. The authors attributed the lower cell density and titer at 5,000 L, compared with their 500 L scale-down runs, to inadequate mixing <a href="#ref-10">[10]</a>. An earlier cell-free study of 5,000 L bioreactors by the same group had measured longer mixing times than at bench scale and found that pH and DO gradients may exist <a href="#ref-40">[40]</a>. However, each prediction starts from the previous day's measurements, so the agreement at both scales does not show that the rate laws capture that mixing effect. The published validation therefore tests one-day dynamics rather than whole-run prediction from inoculation.

Identifiability limits what any of these fits can mean. Kotidis et al. described their parameter estimation problem as structurally unidentifiable, because below saturation the data determine only the ratio of a maximum rate to its half-saturation constant <a href="#ref-12">[12]</a>. They therefore constrained most half-saturation constants to within an order of magnitude of the observed concentrations, and cautioned that the estimated values are not intended to represent the actual kinetic parameters <a href="#ref-12">[12]</a>. Sietaram et al. took the opposite route, fitting 13 parameters separately to each of 656 ambr 15 runs, which characterizes each clone but does not test prediction <a href="#ref-13">[13]</a>. Notably, their model could not reproduce the drop in ammonium that some cell lines showed around the stationary phase, because its ammonium rate is proportional to growth <a href="#ref-13">[13]</a>. In contrast, the kinetic-oriented flux model of Khare et al. predicted lactate and alanine switching from production to consumption, as well as fluctuating ammonia <a href="#ref-15">[15]</a>. Machine learning has also been applied to the calibration itself. Kim et al. let a language model propose how to adjust the parameter bounds of a mechanistic CHO model. However, each of the eight batches was calibrated independently, so the result is again a set of separate fits <a href="#ref-41">[41]</a>.

Few of the mechanistic models respond to physical culture conditions. Among the mechanistic models in the table above, only the model of Reddy et al. includes pH <a href="#ref-16">[16]</a>, and none includes temperature or DO. A dynamic flux balance model with kinetic constraints written as functions of pH and temperature was recently validated on 20 ambr 250 fed-batch experiments <a href="#ref-42">[42]</a>. Learned models take such signals as inputs more readily. Bayer et al.'s hybrid model takes temperature <a href="#ref-11">[11]</a>, and the pooled network of Smiatek et al. takes pH and temperature <a href="#ref-27">[27]</a>. The model of Wang et al. takes pH and specific oxygen uptake. The latter divides the oxygen uptake rate by viable cell density. The study reports daily offline cell counts, and the released code reads a prepared series for each test run <a href="#ref-35">[35]</a>.

Yu et al. provide the clearest demonstration of filtered transfer. They started from the Kotidis et al. parameters for CHO-T127 in shake flasks. A dual ensemble Kalman filter, with 50 or 75 members and no covariance inflation, then adapted the parameters as measurements arrived <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. The filter reconstructed bioreactor cultures at 36.5 °C and after a shift to 32 °C, as well as cultures of a second cell line, CHO-GS46, under three feeds <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. For CHO-GS46 with Feed C, its mean absolute error stayed within 1.5 times that of a full refit to the same data for every state, and fell below it for ammonia, lactate and glutamate <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. Their demonstration measured all eight states, and the authors explain that without observing all states, parameter corrections become underdetermined <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. For CHO-GS46 with Feed U plus 40%, their forecasts improved as more data were assimilated. However, those from days 7 and 9 missed a sharp late decline in viable cells that had not yet appeared in the data <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. In a second study, the group used an ensemble Kalman filter to infer intracellular nucleotide sugars from extracellular measurements in four independent experiments. The authors report that the open-loop model showed substantial mismatch, and that the filter corrected the key metabolic profiles <a href="#ref-55">[55]</a>.

The parameter trajectories are as informative as the fits. When a filter has to keep moving a parameter to follow a new run, the corresponding rate law may be missing a mechanism. In Yu et al.'s results, the biomass yields on glutamine and ammonia rose from day 6 in both bioreactor runs <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. Both lactate activation constants moved after the shift to 32 °C and under all three feeds for CHO-GS46. In addition, the ammonia inhibition constant rose sharply at the onset of the stationary phase under the two asparagine-rich feeds <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. The authors are explicit about the limit of this approach. When a target system involves phenomena that the model structurally lacks, such as mass-transfer limitations at pilot or manufacturing scale, the unmodeled dynamics are spread across the existing parameters. The resulting values then cannot be meaningfully interpreted <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. The shift to 32 °C illustrates the milder case, in which the missing temperature dependence was absorbed by shifts in the growth and productivity parameters <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>.

Other approaches also update or compare parameters between scales. Hernández Rodríguez et al. re-estimated growth parameters with moving horizon estimation across three consecutive scales of an industrial CHO seed train, from 40 to 2,160 L. By including prior knowledge, they obtained useful estimates from as few as two sampling points <a href="#ref-45">[45]</a>. Arndt et al. instead compared parameter distributions between scales. Between a 4 mL microtiter plate and a 3.75 L bioreactor, they found differences in glucose-related parameters with little effect on the simulated dynamics. The growth and antibody-formation parameters agreed. In contrast, a 2 mL deep-well plate and a 40 mL shake flask differed significantly, and the authors bridged that gap by transferring the parameter distributions and confirming the resulting predictions experimentally <a href="#ref-46">[46]</a>. Kotidis et al. cautioned that unstructured models are usually limited to the cell line and data used to build them. As a first step toward a new system, they proposed a sensitivity analysis to select the parameters to re-estimate <a href="#ref-12">[12]</a>. Kotidis and Kontoravdi then reparameterized the model for a second cell line by refitting only the parameters that a global sensitivity analysis identified as influential <a href="#ref-43">[43]</a>. Yu et al. later used this kind of refit as the benchmark for their filter <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. Craven et al. asked directly whether a Monod model and its parameters carry across scales and culture modes. The experimentally determined parameters, which influenced the model most, changed with scale and mode but were easily calculated <a href="#ref-44">[44]</a>. Ben Yahia et al. instead kept the parameters of a piecewise linear model from 2 L cultures and predicted metabolic rates at 2,000 L from the measured growth rates <a href="#ref-47">[47]</a>.

Much of the machine-learning transfer between products rests on simulated data. Helleckes et al. showed that meta learning and one-hot encoding with Gaussian processes roughly halved test error compared with models trained only on the new product, when two, four or eight new-product runs were available. In addition, a designed set of calibration runs matched standard experimental designs with about four times fewer experiments, although all of these results came from simulated mammalian cell culture data <a href="#ref-50">[50]</a>. Hutter et al. likewise compared learned product embeddings with one-hot encoding on a simulated cross-product task <a href="#ref-51">[51]</a>. A preprint by Martens et al. extends this line to joint transfer across scales and clones, again in simulation <a href="#ref-56">[56]</a>. Sun et al. applied multi-fidelity Gaussian processes to measured scale-up and cell-line datasets. In the cross-line case, they used 30 source cultures and three, five or seven of nine labeled target cultures. The model forecast endpoint titer using mid-run measurements <a href="#ref-54">[54]</a>.

Several recent studies use measured culture data, mostly from CHO cells. Yatipanthalawa et al. built hybrid models that predict next-day viable cell density, titer and cumulative glucose consumption. The models performed well on a dataset for a new product without further training, and retraining on part of the new data improved the predictions of viable cell density and titer <a href="#ref-52">[52]</a>. In a follow-up, hybrid and purely data-driven models were trained on a narrow set of process data and tested on four other process datasets. The error of recursive predictions over twelve working days correlated strongly with the dissimilarity between the training and test data <a href="#ref-57">[57]</a>. Gadiyar et al. trained a Gaussian-process hybrid model on 24 perfusion runs of one CHO-K1 clone. They then controlled cultures of a new clone with the same model, which carried a categorical input for the clone and was retrained each day <a href="#ref-53">[53]</a>. In addition, Smiatek et al. pooled development-scale runs of four antibodies from one platform process in a single recurrent network. Measured titer, cell densities and viability of a fifth antibody, which was not used in training, lay mostly within the standard deviation of the network's simulations at three temperatures <a href="#ref-27">[27]</a>. Karimi Alavijeh et al. compiled CHO antibody scale-up data from the literature and found that an embedding layer improved neural-network predictions of cell growth at large scale <a href="#ref-58">[58]</a>. These results are encouraging, but their test designs differ. They include next-day predictions, models retrained during the run and models pooled across the systems being compared, so the gain from transfer alone is hard to isolate.

Other studies target scale-up, cell line selection or culture mode directly. Li et al. generated 26 runs at 2 L and five at 80 L using a simulator calibrated to published data. They adapted the model on four simulated target runs per fold and held out the fifth. The physics-encoded model gave a mean normalized RMSE of 0.081 compared to 0.086 for conventional fine-tuning, with a slightly higher product RMSE <a href="#ref-59">[59]</a>. For cell line selection, Facco et al. used latent-variable methods, including joint-Y projection to latent structures, to compare cell lines across scales and to predict performance at another scale <a href="#ref-60">[60]</a>. Three preprints report tests on measured data. Ma et al. encoded clones and scales as categorical inputs of a multi-objective Bayesian optimizer, which they validated on historical CHO datasets and in small-scale bioreactors <a href="#ref-61">[61]</a>. Sietaram et al. trained a hybrid model on 140 historical cell lines and tested it on 15 clones from a separate campaign. The inputs included earlier small-scale measurements. For the 15 clones, the squared correlations between predicted and measured values were 0.80 for viable cell concentration and 0.88 for titer. Normalized RMSE was 31% for glucose, 48% for glutamine and 82% for lactate <a href="#ref-62">[62]</a>. Monteiro et al. trained a model on CHO fed-batch data that predicts fluxes from measured concentrations under intracellular and reactor mass balances. Among the retraining strategies they compared for continuous culture, retraining on limited continuous data worked best <a href="#ref-48">[48]</a>.

The transfer-learning study of Rogers et al. used microalgal lutein production, not mammalian cells, but its lessons still apply to CHO models <a href="#ref-63">[63]</a>. In their first case study, retraining only the output layer of a neural network outperformed retraining two layers, because the earlier layers held kinetics worth keeping. In both case studies, retraining all parameters under a penalty that kept them close to the source model was the most accurate strategy by a small margin. This approach is the same idea as re-estimating a kinetic model around published values. The advantage over models built only from target data also diminished as the number of target datasets rose from one to three <a href="#ref-63">[63]</a>. The same group later combined transfer learning with hybrid models. They adapted a hybrid model of one *Chlorella* strain to a newly engineered strain with minimal data <a href="#ref-64">[64]</a>. They also adapted a laboratory-scale hybrid model of a yeast fermentation with pilot-scale data alone, and predicted the industrial scale with a mean absolute percentage error of 23.2% <a href="#ref-65">[65]</a>.

In the Yu et al. study, the filter followed new cultures by correcting the states and parameters with measurements from those cultures <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>. The studies that carry a model forward with fixed parameters address a separate question. Their results depend on which inputs are known, when they become available and which outputs are being predicted.

## Designing media and feeds with machine learning

Media and feed design uses a model to select inputs for the next experiment. Several studies trained a model on tested formulations, selected one or more new candidates and compared them in culture with a reference medium or another search method. The resulting experiments test whether the selection improves a specified outcome. A comparison of the measured response with the value predicted before the experiment tests numerical accuracy.

Early studies screened components statistically and used a learned model to rank them. Xu et al. combined a screening design of seven supplements with support vector regression, and found zinc sulfate, transferrin and albumin to be the influential ones for a CHO suspension culture <a href="#ref-66">[66]</a>. Zou et al. replaced the linear step with nonparametric regression under a Gaussian kernel, which identified components that partial least squares had missed in media and feed development <a href="#ref-75">[75]</a>. Gangwar et al. ranked seven metal ions using 34 observations, then selected an iron and zinc formulation to approach a reference charge variant profile. In the fed-batch test, the acidic fraction moved close to the reference, whereas the basic fraction moved farther from it (13.53% with supplementation compared to 12.86% in the control and 11.41% in the reference). Specific productivity was similar in the supplemented and control cultures <a href="#ref-67">[67]</a>.

More recent work closes the loop over several rounds. Hashizume et al. tuned 44 components of a serum-free medium for an IgG-producing CHO line, alternating designed experiments with model predictions. Notably, properties they had not set out to control, such as osmolality and the amino acid balance, entered the search as the rounds went on <a href="#ref-76">[76]</a>. In addition, the same group fine-tuned a 57-component medium for CHO-K1 over 364 tested media, and reported about 60% higher cell concentration than commercial alternatives <a href="#ref-68">[68]</a>. Ndahiro et al. tuned their search on a kinetic model and bounded the candidate amino acid concentrations by solubility. The two highest day-5 titers among the Bayesian proposals were 2.80 and 2.55 g/L, compared to 1.45 g/L for the highest-titer space-filling condition. Two Bayesian proposals yielded less than 0.2 g/L. The comparison therefore shows both the gains and the variability of the search <a href="#ref-69">[69]</a>.

Two studies outside CHO are worth noting for their design. Cosenza et al. optimized a 14-component medium for a murine myoblast line with a multi-information-source method. They reported 181% more cells than a commercial medium of similar cost, in 38% fewer experiments than an efficient design-of-experiments benchmark <a href="#ref-77">[77]</a>. Narayanan et al. applied an iterative Bayesian framework to cytokine supplementation for human blood cells and to recombinant protein production in yeast. They reported improvements with 3 to 30 times fewer experiments than standard designs would need. They then widened the design space with five further additives and carried the earlier data over through transfer learning <a href="#ref-78">[78]</a>. Benchmarks support the choice of method. Across six published media datasets, Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization found hidden optima in fewer evaluations than classical designs or random search, although none of those datasets came from CHO cells <a href="#ref-79">[79]</a>.

A second line of work uses metabolic models rather than learned ones to decide what to add. Fouladiha et al. passed the exchange reactions of a CHO genome-scale model through a screening design and a response surface. They reported about a two-fold increase in total antibody from threonine and arachidonate <a href="#ref-71">[71]</a>. Hong et al. combined multivariate analysis with genome-scale simulation, traced the poorest growth condition to a redox imbalance in the TCA cycle, and confirmed experimentally that coenzyme Q10 supplementation improved growth <a href="#ref-80">[80]</a>. The same group later applied the framework to two feed media, adding glutamate to one and asparagine to the other, and improved growth, culture longevity or titer <a href="#ref-81">[81]</a>. Yeo et al. paired partial least squares with the flux balance analysis of a genome-scale model and identified four nutrients and two waste products at limiting levels <a href="#ref-82">[82]</a>. In addition, Pang et al. used a genome-scale model to set the asparagine to aspartate ratio of a feed, and found that the cells preferred asparagine <a href="#ref-73">[73]</a>. Salim et al. took the stoichiometric route without a genome-scale model, pairing multivariate analysis with amino acid balances, and reported 55% more total cell growth and about 80% more total antibody <a href="#ref-72">[72]</a>.

Hybrid models bring the two lines together. Negahban et al. blended three commercial media and searched run to run with a partial least squares model coupled to dynamic flux balance analysis. A gradient correction step steered the search, and specific productivity rose more than 20% above the best single medium <a href="#ref-70">[70]</a>. Product quality can be the objective as well as titer. Walsh et al. trained models of final titer and glycan metrics on the initial medium composition and process parameters, then searched a surrogate for a new combination. The proposed condition lowered mannosylation by about 10% and raised titer <a href="#ref-74">[74]</a>.

Most formulation tests used the same cell line and scale as the development experiments. Hashizume and Ying also tested seven CHO-K1-optimized media on human HeLa-S3 cells in biological triplicate. Cell concentrations were similar to or lower than those in the two eRDF controls <a href="#ref-68">[68]</a>. This leaves open whether an optimized medium would retain its benefit in a second CHO clone. An example from stoichiometric media design is the study of Kuwae et al., who developed a basal medium and feeds with one CHO line and reported titers in three additional lines <a href="#ref-83">[83]</a>. Several reviews cover the algorithms and their trade-offs in more detail <a href="#ref-84">[84]</a>, <a href="#ref-85">[85]</a>, <a href="#ref-86">[86]</a>, and one surveys model-based choices of amino acid composition <a href="#ref-87">[87]</a>.

## Transferring sensor calibrations

Models can only be judged as well as the measurements allow, and in-line sensors supply a growing share of fed-batch data. Their calibrations are themselves machine-learning models, usually partial least squares regressions on Raman spectra, so they face the same transfer problem as the cell models. Generic Raman models measure glucose, lactate, ammonium and viable cell density with useful accuracy, but their relative error is largest at low concentrations, where late-culture metabolism has to be judged.

Soft sensors built on other signals face the same question. Jossen et al. related cumulative oxygen consumption to lactate-adjusted glucose use in 58 CHO fed-batch cultures from 10 L to 2,000 L. They found linear correlations with R² of at least 0.984 across projects, cell lines and scales <a href="#ref-117">[117]</a>. Other soft sensors combine a calibration with a process model. Ohadi et al. passed fluorescence-based estimates to a dynamic metabolic model in an extended Kalman filter <a href="#ref-118">[118]</a>. In addition, Narayanan et al. coupled a hybrid model to the same kind of filter and improved monitoring accuracy by at least 35% over benchmarks based on partial least squares <a href="#ref-119">[119]</a>.

Overall, calibrations have transferred most reliably for glucose and lactate. Cell density and titer models were more sensitive to scale and clone <a href="#ref-101">[101]</a>, <a href="#ref-98">[98]</a>, and late-culture concentrations and the nitrogen balance still need offline assays.

## Summary

Frozen-parameter models have predicted CHO cultures with new feeds, initial conditions and culture modes <a href="#ref-12">[12]</a>, <a href="#ref-15">[15]</a>, <a href="#ref-9">[9]</a>, <a href="#ref-16">[16]</a>. At the largest scale steps in this review, the models used measurements from each test run <a href="#ref-11">[11]</a>, <a href="#ref-47">[47]</a>. When a perfusion model was carried from ambr 250 to a 5 L working volume, the predictions deviated after day 12.5 and the authors added retention terms and refitted parameters <a href="#ref-31">[31]</a>. Filtering can reconstruct measured trajectories in a new culture. Parameter drift may indicate that the model is absorbing the effect of a process it does not describe, as in the temperature-shift case of Yu et al. <a href="#ref-7">[7]</a>.

Within-process tests remain common among the hybrid and data-driven studies reviewed here <a href="#ref-23">[23]</a>, <a href="#ref-27">[27]</a>, <a href="#ref-20">[20]</a>, <a href="#ref-36">[36]</a>. Tests across systems include simulation, next-day prediction, and adaptation during a measured run <a href="#ref-50">[50]</a>, <a href="#ref-59">[59]</a>, <a href="#ref-52">[52]</a>, <a href="#ref-53">[53]</a>. Growth and titer can also transfer more readily than metabolite trajectories. In Sietaram et al.’s independent 15-clone test, the squared correlation for titer was 0.88 and the normalized error for lactate was 82% <a href="#ref-62">[62]</a>.

Of the sensor calibrations reviewed, glucose and lactate generally transferred more readily than cell density or titer <a href="#ref-101">[101]</a>, <a href="#ref-98">[98]</a>, <a href="#ref-104">[104]</a>. Errors near depletion still warrant offline checks <a href="#ref-88">[88]</a>, <a href="#ref-89">[89]</a>, <a href="#ref-90">[90]</a>. In media and feed design, prospective experiments show whether selected formulations improve a specified outcome <a href="#ref-69">[69]</a>, <a href="#ref-70">[70]</a>. The CHO-K1 media study reported a growth advantage in CHO-K1 that did not carry over to HeLa-S3 <a href="#ref-68">[68]</a>.
