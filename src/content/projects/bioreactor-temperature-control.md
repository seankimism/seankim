Keeping a cell culture at its target temperature means balancing heat added by equipment and cells against heat leaving the vessel. As a process grows from a few hundred milliliters to thousands of liters, both that balance and the response of the controller change.

I built this project from the heat-transfer model outward: first the vessel and media warm-up, then temperature control, and finally the heat generated by growing cells. A simplified Cytiva XDR-2000 L single-use bioreactor provides the large-scale example. Replaying the same CHO fed-batch and perfusion cell-density histories in a 200 mL ambr 250 culture, a 2 L culture in a 3 L glass vessel, and a 2,000 L culture connects the model to a practical scale-up question: **when is turning down the heater enough, and when is active cooling needed?**

Under the selected assumptions, both bench vessels hold either culture profile without active cooling. At 2,000 L, the fed-batch case comes close to the amount of heat the vessel can lose to the room, while perfusion requires active cooling. These are illustrative thermal predictions; calibration and experimental validation remain the next steps.

## How heat moves through the vessel

The thermal model connects circulating jacket water, the steel wall, culture medium, headspace air, and the surrounding room. The medium is assumed to be well mixed, with a uniform temperature throughout the liquid. The headspace also has one bulk temperature, while the wall temperature varies with height.

Heat passes from the jacket water through the steel wall and single-use bag to the medium. Above the liquid level, the wall exchanges heat with headspace air. Neighboring sections of steel also exchange heat, and exposed surfaces lose heat to the room. The bag film and its contact with the steel resist heat flow; the model includes that resistance without assigning the bag a separate temperature.

## Two temperature measurements, two control loops

The next layer adds a temperature-control unit (TCU) and two connected controllers. The outer controller compares culture temperature with its setpoint and sets a target for the jacket temperature. The faster inner controller compares jacket temperature with that target and commands the TCU. Water recirculates between the TCU and the jacket, and the modeled jacket temperature represents the mixed water there: its response reflects circulation and heat exchange with the vessel, while the culture responds as heat passes through the wall and bag.

Both control modes use this cascade structure. In heating-only operation the TCU can only add heat, and the minimum heater power is zero. Heating and cooling adds the ability to remove heat from the circulating water. The figures label the two modes "Heating only" and "Heating + cooling".

Holding cell-free medium at 36.5 °C takes about **0.75 kW** of TCU heating in this model. The relevant limit for cell-generated heat is slightly lower: about **0.72 kW**, calculated with the culture held at 36.5 °C and TCU power set to zero. The distinction matters because heat entering through the TCU and heat generated inside the culture produce different jacket and wall temperatures, and therefore different losses to the 20 °C room.

## Explore the temperature response

Adjust the proportional gain (P) and integral time (T<sub>i</sub>) of the outer culture controller. Higher P produces a larger jacket-target adjustment for the same temperature error; shorter T<sub>i</sub> strengthens the correction for persistent error. Each slider position is a multiple of the reference tuning for the selected volume, from 0.25× to 3×. The inner jacket controller stays at its reference tuning.

This cell-free example warms medium from 4 to 36.5 °C, holds, then changes the target to 33 °C at 16 hours over a 36-hour run. Compare heating-only operation, which can cool only by passive heat loss, with heating and cooling at 400, 1,000, and 2,000 L. Watch for overshoot after each target change and for how long the culture stays outside the ±0.2 °C band.

At the reference tuning and 2,000 L, the warm-up is limited by the equipment rather than the controller: the heater runs at its 20 kW limit and the supply water at its 40 °C cap for most of the climb, and the culture settles within the band about 7 hours after the start. The two modes are identical until the target drops. Heating and cooling then brings the culture within ±0.2 °C of 33 °C in about 3 hours, while heating-only operation takes about 15 hours because the vessel can only lose heat to the room. The biological comparison below holds a constant target throughout.

## Metabolic heat: fed-batch and perfusion

As viable cell density increases, metabolic heat can turn a heating requirement into a cooling requirement. Two published CHO profiles provide different loads for the same 2,000 L thermal model. Both use an assumed 20 pW of metabolic heat per viable cell, informed by direct CHO320 calorimetry reported by [Guan and Kemp (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008038515285). This is a shared modeling assumption, not a measured rate for either culture.

## Fed-batch: the heater backs off as the cells grow

At 20 pW per cell, both control modes hold 36.5 °C throughout the 17-day fed-batch profile; the deviation is not visible at the scale of the ±0.2 °C band. The response shows in the jacket. As metabolic heat rises toward its 716 W peak, the heater backs off and the jacket water settles about 0.6 °C below the culture, so heat flows from the culture through the wall to the jacket and on to the room. The peak is less than 1% below the approximately 722 W heater-off limit, and the heater contributes almost nothing at peak density. The two responses overlap because cooling is never called.

The margin is thin. This case stays heating-only because its peak load happens to fall just below the modeled passive loss. A modestly higher per-cell heat rate, a warmer room, or a higher peak density would cross the line. The result shows that heating-only operation is sufficient for this particular load, not for fed-batch in general.

## Perfusion: cooling is required

The perfusion case holds a 36.5 °C setpoint for its 28-day observation window. The same 20 pW per cell produces a much larger load because there are many more cells: metabolic heat passes the approximately 0.72 kW heater-off limit around day 6, near 18 million cells/mL, and peaks above 4 kW. Once the load exceeds what the vessel loses to the room, turning off the heater cannot hold the culture. In heating-only operation the culture leaves the setpoint shortly after day 6 and reaches the 40 °C simulation stop on day 7.7; the run ends there because the source VCD history, measured at 36.5 °C, says nothing about cells that have overheated. With cooling available, the culture stays within the band for the full 28 days while the jacket water falls to about 33 °C to carry the peak load away.

## Bench scale: the same cell profiles, different heat paths

The bench comparison keeps the same 17-day fed-batch and 28-day perfusion histories, the same assumed 20 pW per viable cell, and the same constant 36.5 °C target. **Culture volume stays fixed at 2 L and 200 mL for both processes.** Feed additions, volume changes, and feed/harvest heat are excluded, matching the 2,000 L thermal replay. Each culture starts at controlled equilibrium so that metabolic heat is the only changing load.

### How the bench controllers work

The bench models use a single controller responding to culture temperature. The 3 L glass vessel receives heat through an external blanket; the ambr 250 receives heat through a holder block in contact with its tapered polycarbonate vessel. The controller changes element power directly, rather than setting a jacket-water target for a second loop. Exposed surfaces and the element exchange heat with the room, including the exposed base of the 3 L vessel.

## Why cooling becomes a scale-up requirement

Small vessels have more surface area for each liter of culture. That gives heat a larger area to escape through relative to the amount of medium being kept warm. At the same cell density, a larger culture generates proportionally more heat, while the vessel's surface area grows more slowly. Cooling from the surrounding room therefore becomes less effective per liter as scale increases.

The last column below answers a practical question: **how much heat can the cells generate before active cooling is needed?** It shows the heat each modeled vessel can release to a 20 °C room while the culture stays at 36.5 °C, with the heater and active cooling both off, after temperatures have stabilized. The values are watts per liter of culture (W/L). They account for the modeled wall, heating element or jacket, and headspace heat paths.

The shared cell histories generate peak loads of **0.358 W/L for fed-batch** and **2.04 W/L for perfusion**. Both are below the heat the bench vessels can release to the room, so reducing heater power is enough to hold temperature. At 2,000 L, the fed-batch load nearly uses up that ability; perfusion generates heat faster than it can escape, so active cooling must remove the excess.

The vessels also differ in shape, material, and heat path. Their numerical differences reflect all of those factors; the surface-area column alone does not determine how much heat reaches the room.

<details class="xdr-details">
  <summary>How geometry and heat transfer set this balance</summary>

For geometrically similar vessels, area grows with length squared and volume with length cubed: $A/V \propto V^{-1/3}$. Increasing volume tenfold leaves each liter with about 46% of the original surface area. At the same cell density and per-cell metabolism, total heat generation grows directly with volume.

Heat removal also depends on the heat-transfer coefficient and the temperature difference to the room. The relation $\dot Q = UA\Delta T$ captures that dependence; wall, contact, and fluid-film resistances contribute to the overall conductance. The table's heat-loss values come from the full thermal network at steady state. [Heat-transfer basis](https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node123.html).

</details>

A successful heating-only bench run therefore provides limited evidence about cooling needs after scale-up. In this comparison, large-scale perfusion needs installed cooling even though both bench vessels hold the same profile without it. The selected 2,000 L fed-batch case still holds with heating only, but its margin is small enough that a warmer room, more cells, or higher per-cell heat could change the requirement. Cooling capacity should be assessed against the expected culture heat load and required temperature shifts at the intended scale, using calibrated heat-transfer and biological inputs.

## Calibration and experimental validation

The simulations connect vessel heat transfer, control response, and changing cell heat. The next step is to estimate the uncertain thermal parameters and test predictions against independent cell-free experiments. The proposed plan below separates calibration runs from validation experiments. Heating and cooling tests across working volumes, with repeated reference runs, have precedent in [Cytiva's XDR characterization study](https://cdn.cytivalifesciences.com/api/public/content/digi-23201-pdf); this matrix is a proposed study for the XDR model.

### Measurements

Record synchronized liquid temperatures at multiple positions, jacket inlet and outlet temperatures, jacket flow, accessible wall temperatures, headspace temperature, and room temperature. Also record fill volume, agitation, and bag installation. Check sensor offsets and response times before testing. Spatial liquid measurements would test the assumption that one bulk temperature represents the culture medium.

Use measured jacket conditions as boundary inputs for the thermal model. Replaying measured inlet histories remains a planned extension of the constant-inlet warm-up model; the controller explorer calculates its inlet conditions from the modeled TCU and circulation loop.

## Next steps toward a coupled culture model

The current model already includes controller dynamics, three vessel scales, and heat calculated from prescribed cell-density histories. Further development would connect those thermal responses to measured equipment behavior and to the cells' response to temperature.

### Measured equipment response

Refine TCU capacity, sensor response, controller tuning, bag or block contact, and room heat losses using measured data. Test heating, cooling, and temperature shifts across working volumes. This would establish how well the model predicts the available heating and cooling capacity and the time needed to reach a new target.

### Temperature-dependent cell behavior

Estimate metabolic heat from oxygen uptake rate (OUR), initially using roughly **450–500 kJ of heat released per mol O₂ consumed** as an aerobic approximation. Check that estimate against cell-specific metabolism, including heat production not captured by oxygen uptake alone. [Gnaiger and Kemp (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-2728(90)90164-Y) discuss the relationship between heat production, oxygen consumption, and aerobic and anaerobic contributions in cultured mammalian cells.

Couple the heat source to temperature-dependent growth, death, and productivity kinetics. A temperature shift, such as **36.5 to 33 °C**, could then change both heat transfer and cellular behavior. In the current results, cell density is imposed and does not respond to the simulated temperature.

### Changing culture volume

Extend the fixed-volume examples to account for feeding, sampling, and bleeding. A changing culture volume would change the amount of medium being heated and the vessel surface in contact with it. Future simulations would update those quantities and include the heat carried by incoming and outgoing streams.

### Gas transfer and other heat sources

Connect temperature to O₂ and CO₂ solubility, the volumetric mass-transfer coefficient ($k_La$), and CO₂/bicarbonate equilibria and pH. Develop a pCO₂ model as part of this layer, and extend the illustrated heat balances to include agitation heat and evaporative cooling. These connections would allow temperature, gas transfer, and culture behavior to evolve together.
