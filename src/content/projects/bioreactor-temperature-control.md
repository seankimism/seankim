Culture temperature depends on the balance among heat supplied by equipment, metabolic heat generated by the cells, and heat lost to the surroundings. However, increasing culture volume changes the relative contributions of these terms as well as the response of the controller. As such, maintaining temperature at bench scale does not establish the heating and cooling requirements of a larger vessel.

The objective of this project was to investigate how vessel scale and culture conditions influence temperature control. I developed a thermal model of a simplified Cytiva XDR-2000 L single-use bioreactor and (i) simulated media warm-up and cascade controller response, (ii) applied the same published CHO fed-batch and perfusion cell-density histories at three scales, a 200 mL ambr 250 culture, a 2 L culture in a 3 L glass vessel, and a 2,000 L culture, and (iii) modeled cold feed additions to compare temperature recovery as culture volume increased.

Both bench vessels maintained the target temperature for either culture profile without active cooling. Notably, the 2,000 L fed-batch heat load approached the vessel's passive heat-loss capacity, and perfusion exceeded it. Namely, at 20 pW per viable cell, metabolic heat surpassed the approximately 0.72 kW heater-off limit at about 18 million cells/mL, so the perfusion culture required active cooling from day 6. More interestingly, cold feed boluses produced the same initial temperature drop at each scale when the feed-to-culture volume ratio was matched, yet recovery took 10–15 minutes through a bench heating element and 20–35 minutes through the 2,000 L jacket. Overall, these results suggest that heat removal, rather than heat supply, is the critical thermal requirement during scale-up, highlighting the importance of evaluating cooling capacity at the intended scale.

## Heat transfer through the vessel

The model represents heat exchange among circulating jacket water, the steel wall, culture medium, headspace air, and the surrounding room. I treated the medium and headspace as well-mixed compartments with one bulk temperature each, whereas the wall temperature is resolved along the vessel height.

Heat transfer from the jacket to the medium occurs through the steel wall and the single-use bag, while above the liquid level the wall exchanges heat with the headspace. In addition, the model accounts for conduction between neighboring wall sections and for heat loss from exposed surfaces to the room. The bag film and its contact with the steel are represented as thermal resistances rather than as a separate temperature state.

## Warm-up before inoculation

I first simulated cell-free media warm-up with constant jacket-inlet conditions to examine heat transfer before introducing feedback control or metabolic heat.

The reference case contained 1,000 L of medium initially at 4 °C, with jacket water supplied at 40 °C and 50 L/min and room temperature held at 20 °C. Under these conditions, the medium reached 36.5 °C after approximately 5 h 34 min.

<span id="two-temperature-measurements-two-control-loops" aria-hidden="true"></span>

## Cascade temperature control

I then introduced feedback control using a temperature-control unit (TCU) and two controllers arranged in cascade. The outer controller converts the difference between culture temperature and its setpoint into a jacket-temperature target, and the faster inner controller regulates the TCU from the jacket-temperature error. Water recirculates between the TCU and the jacket, where its temperature depends on circulation and heat exchange with the vessel, and the culture responds to the resulting heat transfer through the wall and bag.

I compared two operating modes with the same cascade structure. In heating-only operation, the TCU can supply heat or reduce heater power to zero, whereas in heating and cooling mode it can also remove heat from the circulating water. These modes are labeled "Heating only" and "Heating + cooling" in the figures.

<span id="explore-the-temperature-response" aria-hidden="true"></span>

## Controller response to temperature changes

I examined the effects of controller tuning by varying proportional gain (P) and integral time (T<sub>i</sub>) in the outer culture controller. Increasing P produces a larger jacket-target adjustment for a given temperature error, while decreasing T<sub>i</sub> strengthens integral action on persistent error. In the explorer, each slider ranges from 0.25× to 3× the reference tuning for the selected volume, and the inner jacket controller remains at its reference tuning.

To compare warm-up and cooling responses, I heated cell-free medium from 4 to 36.5 °C, held it, and then decreased the target to 33 °C at 16 hours, with each simulation covering 36 hours. I compared heating-only and heating–cooling operation at 400, 1,000, and 2,000 L, using overshoot and time outside the ±0.2 °C band to assess the response.

At 2,000 L and the reference tuning, the heater operated at its 20 kW limit and the supply water at its 40 °C cap for most of warm-up, so equipment capacity rather than tuning limited the response, and culture temperature settled within the band after about 7 hours. Both control modes gave the same response until the target decreased. Active cooling then returned the culture to within ±0.2 °C of 33 °C in about 3 hours, compared with about 15 hours under heating-only operation, because heat removal in heating-only mode is limited to passive loss to the room. The biological comparisons below use a constant temperature target.

## Heating demand during fed-batch culture

Both control modes maintained 36.5 °C throughout the 14-day fed-batch simulation at 20 pW per cell. Culture-temperature deviations were not visible at the scale of the ±0.2 °C band, although jacket temperature changed with cell density. As metabolic heat approached its 716 W peak, heater power decreased and the jacket water stabilized about 0.6 °C below the culture, which allowed heat to pass from the culture through the wall and jacket to the room. In fact, the peak load was less than 1% below the approximately 722 W heater-off limit, leaving almost no heating demand at peak density. Active cooling was therefore not required, and the two control modes produced overlapping responses.

<span id="perfusion-cooling-is-required" aria-hidden="true"></span>

## Cooling requirement during perfusion culture

The perfusion profile exceeded passive heat-loss capacity around day 6, at approximately 18 million cells/mL. At the same 20 pW per cell, metabolic heat surpassed the approximately 0.72 kW heater-off limit and peaked above 4 kW, so reducing heater power to zero was insufficient to maintain the 36.5 °C setpoint. More interestingly, in heating-only operation the culture temperature departed from the setpoint shortly after day 6 and reached the 40 °C simulation stop on day 7.7, whereas with active cooling it remained within the band for the full 28 days while the jacket water fell to about 33 °C at peak load. I stopped the heating-only simulation at 40 °C because the source VCD history, measured at 36.5 °C, does not describe cellular responses to overheating.

## Bench-scale thermal response

To examine the effect of vessel scale, I applied the same 14-day fed-batch and 28-day perfusion histories at fixed culture volumes of 2 L and 200 mL, again at 20 pW per viable cell and a constant 36.5 °C target. Each culture started at controlled equilibrium with metabolic heat as the only changing load, as in the 2,000 L comparison; feed additions are examined in the [Feeding](#temperature-drop-and-recovery-after-feed-additions) section.

<span id="how-the-bench-controllers-work" aria-hidden="true"></span>

### Bench vessel control

Each bench model uses a single controller that regulates element power directly from culture temperature, without a second loop for jacket-water temperature. The 3 L glass vessel receives heat through an external blanket, whereas the ambr 250 receives heat through a holder block contacting its tapered polycarbonate vessel. The element and exposed vessel surfaces, including the base of the 3 L vessel, exchange heat with the room.

### Fed-batch at bench scale

Neither bench culture required active cooling during the 14-day fed-batch simulation. Peak metabolic heat was about 0.716 W at 2 L and 0.0716 W at 200 mL, with maximum culture-temperature errors below 0.001 °C. As metabolic heat increased, the controller reduced heating power and the blanket or block temperature decreased, but heating power remained positive because passive heat-loss capacity far exceeded the metabolic load at both bench scales. In contrast, the same profile at 2,000 L approached the passive heat-loss limit.

### Perfusion at bench scale

The high-density perfusion profile also remained within the hold band at both bench scales, with maximum culture-temperature errors below 0.01 °C. Peak metabolic heat reached 4.08 W at 2 L and 0.408 W at 200 mL, and both controllers supplied heat throughout the run without active cooling. In contrast, the same cell density and per-cell heat produced 4.08 kW at 2,000 L, exceeding passive heat-loss capacity at 36.5 °C by more than fivefold.

## Temperature drop and recovery after feed additions

Cold feed additions increase culture volume while transiently reducing medium temperature. I used the day-6 bolus in the 14-day NISTCHO fed-batch simulation to compare this response across the ambr 250, the 3 L glass vessel, and the XDR-2000, with blue and orange curves representing feed temperatures of 4 °C and 20 °C, respectively.

At the same feed-to-culture volume ratio, the initial temperature drop was independent of vessel scale: about 0.89 °C for 4 °C feed and 0.45 °C for 20 °C feed. However, recovery differed markedly between the bench vessels and the XDR-2000. Both bench vessels returned to the ±0.2 °C band in about 15 minutes with 4 °C feed and 10 minutes with 20 °C feed, whereas the XDR-2000 required approximately 35 and 20 minutes. Such slower recovery most likely reflects the lower heating capacity per liter of the 2,000 L jacket. Warmer feed therefore reduced both the initial temperature drop and the time outside the band.

## Cooling requirements during scale-up

The difference in cooling requirements reflects the balance between metabolic heat generation and passive heat loss at each scale. Both bench vessels maintained the target without active cooling, whereas the 2,000 L fed-batch load approached the passive heat-loss limit and perfusion exceeded it. Such a difference most likely originates from the surface-area-to-volume ratio: at the same cell density, metabolic heat increases in proportion to culture volume, while surface area increases more slowly, so passive heat loss per liter decreases with scale.

Namely, the prescribed cell-density histories generate peak loads of 0.358 W/L for fed-batch and 2.04 W/L for perfusion. Both are well below the bench vessels' passive heat-loss capacities of 5.60 and 14.4 W/L, allowing temperature control through reduced heater power alone, whereas at 2,000 L the fed-batch load approaches the 0.361 W/L capacity and perfusion exceeds it more than fivefold.

However, the vessels also differ in geometry, material, and heat-transfer path, so their heat-loss capacities reflect these combined effects rather than surface area alone.

<details class="xdr-details">
  <summary>Geometry and heat-transfer relationships</summary>

For geometrically similar vessels, area scales with length squared and volume with length cubed: $A/V \propto V^{-1/3}$. A tenfold increase in volume reduces surface area per liter to about 46% of its original value. At the same cell density and per-cell metabolism, total heat generation increases directly with volume.

Heat removal also depends on the heat-transfer coefficient and the temperature difference to the room, as expressed by $\dot Q = UA\Delta T$. Wall, contact, and fluid-film resistances contribute to the overall conductance. The table's heat-loss values are calculated from the full thermal network at steady state. Collectively, these results show that a process that never needs cooling at bench scale can require it at 2,000 L, and that the fed-batch case sits close enough to the passive limit that a warmer room, higher cell density, or greater per-cell heat could push it over. Cooling requirements should therefore be evaluated at the intended scale against the expected thermal load and required temperature shifts, using calibrated heat-transfer and biological inputs.

## Calibration and experimental validation

The limitations of this study arise from the simplifications required to build the model without vessel-specific measurements. While the geometry, material properties, TCU capacity, water inventory, piping, and sensor positions are schematic inputs rather than measured values, the model preserves the heat-transfer paths that distinguish a jacketed 2,000 L vessel from a bench heating element or block. In addition, the 20 pW per cell rate was not measured for either source culture, the cell-density histories are prescribed and do not respond to the simulated temperature, and the feed simulations assume instantaneous, complete mixing while sampling, bleeding, and perfusion harvest streams are not modeled. The small calculated temperature errors at bench scale therefore reflect idealized thermal responses rather than sensor accuracy or equipment performance, and the fed-batch result applies to the specified thermal conditions rather than to fed-batch cultures in general. As such, vessel-specific calibration and independent measurements are needed to evaluate these predictions.

The proposed study uses cell-free heating and cooling experiments to estimate uncertain parameters, followed by separate runs to test predictions. Heating and cooling tests across working volumes, with repeated reference runs, have precedent in [Cytiva's XDR characterization study](https://cdn.cytivalifesciences.com/api/public/content/digi-23201-pdf), and the experiment matrix below applies this approach to the XDR model.

### Measurements

Liquid temperature is recorded at multiple positions to test whether a single bulk temperature adequately represents the culture medium, and synchronized with jacket inlet and outlet temperatures, jacket flow, accessible wall temperatures, headspace temperature, and room temperature. Fill volume, agitation, and bag installation are documented, and sensor offsets and response times are checked before testing.

Measured jacket conditions then provide boundary inputs for the thermal model. Applying measured inlet histories is a planned extension of the constant-inlet warm-up model; the controller explorer currently calculates inlet conditions from the modeled TCU and circulation loop.

Commissioning begins with water or a defined aqueous surrogate, and subsequent experiments evaluate predictions with representative medium of measured thermal properties, since water-based calibration alone does not establish accuracy for every medium. The proposed temperatures and flow changes depend on the installed temperature-control unit's capability.

The same approach applies to the bench vessels, where blanket or block temperatures, vessel contact, and exposure to the room would test the heat-loss assumptions used in the scale comparison.

## Coupling temperature and culture dynamics

The current simulations impose cell-density histories independently of the predicted temperature, so they cannot predict how a temperature change affects the culture and, in turn, its metabolic heat. Coupling these responses requires temperature-dependent cellular kinetics together with measured equipment behavior. On the equipment side, measurements of TCU capacity, sensor response, controller tuning, bag or block contact, and room heat losses would constrain the model, and heating, cooling, and temperature-shift experiments across working volumes would test the predicted thermal capacity and the time required to reach a new target.

On the biological side, metabolic heat could be estimated from oxygen uptake rate (OUR), initially using roughly 450–500 kJ of heat released per mol O₂ consumed as an aerobic approximation. [Gnaiger and Kemp (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-2728(90)90164-Y) discuss the relationship between heat production, oxygen consumption, and aerobic and anaerobic contributions in cultured mammalian cells, and this estimate would need to be evaluated against cell-specific metabolism, including heat production not captured by oxygen uptake alone. Coupling metabolic heat to temperature-dependent growth, death, and productivity kinetics would then allow a shift from 36.5 to 33 °C, for example, to change both heat transfer and culture kinetics. Finally, sampling, bleeding, feeds delivered over a finite time, and perfusion inlet and harvest streams would add volume and sensible-heat terms to the culture energy balance, agitation heat and evaporative cooling would add further terms, and temperature-dependent O₂ and CO₂ solubility, the volumetric mass-transfer coefficient ($k_La$), and CO₂/bicarbonate equilibria would connect the thermal model with gas transfer, pCO₂, and pH. The resulting model could be used to examine how a temperature change affects both gas transfer and the thermal load generated by the culture.
