Culture temperature depends on heat supplied by equipment, metabolic heat generation, and heat loss to the surroundings. Increasing culture volume changes the relative contributions of these terms and the response of the controller. Therefore, maintaining temperature at bench scale does not establish the heating and cooling requirements of a larger vessel.

The objective of this project was to investigate how vessel scale and culture conditions influence temperature control. A thermal model was developed for a simplified Cytiva XDR-2000 L single-use bioreactor. Media warm-up and controller response were evaluated before applying the same CHO fed-batch and perfusion cell-density histories at three scales. These comparisons included a 200 mL ambr 250 culture, a 2 L culture in a 3 L glass vessel, and a 2,000 L culture. Feed additions were modeled separately to examine temperature recovery as culture volume increased.

Under the selected assumptions, both bench vessels maintained the target temperature for either culture profile without active cooling. In contrast, the 2,000 L fed-batch heat load approached the vessel's passive heat-loss capacity, while perfusion required active cooling. Cold feed boluses produced the same initial temperature drop at each scale when the feed-to-culture volume ratio was matched. However, recovery required 10–15 minutes through a bench heating element and 20–35 minutes through the 2,000 L jacket. These results describe simulated thermal responses; calibration and independent experiments are required to assess their accuracy.

## Heat transfer through the vessel

The model represents heat exchange among circulating jacket water, the steel wall, culture medium, headspace air, and the surrounding room. The medium and headspace are each assumed to be well mixed and are assigned one bulk temperature. In contrast, wall temperature is resolved along the vessel height.

Heat transfer from the jacket to the medium occurs through the steel wall and single-use bag. Above the liquid level, the wall exchanges heat with the headspace. The model also accounts for conduction between neighboring wall sections and heat loss from exposed surfaces to the room. The bag film and its contact with the steel are represented as thermal resistances, without a separate bag-temperature state.

## Warm-up before inoculation

Cell-free media warm-up was simulated with constant jacket-inlet conditions to examine heat transfer before introducing feedback control or metabolic heat generation.

The reference case contained 1,000 L of medium initially at 4 °C, with jacket water supplied at 40 °C and 50 L/min. Room temperature was held at 20 °C. Under these conditions, the simulated medium reached 36.5 °C after approximately 5 h 34 min.

## Cascade temperature control

Feedback control was introduced using a temperature-control unit (TCU) and two controllers arranged in cascade. The outer controller uses the difference between culture temperature and its setpoint to determine a jacket-temperature target. The faster inner controller uses the jacket-temperature error to regulate the TCU. Water recirculates between the TCU and jacket, where its temperature depends on circulation and heat exchange with the vessel. The culture responds to the resulting heat transfer through the wall and bag.

Two operating modes were compared using the same cascade structure. In heating-only operation, the TCU can supply heat or reduce heater power to zero. In the heating and cooling mode, the TCU can also remove heat from the circulating water. These modes are labeled "Heating only" and "Heating + cooling" in the figures.

## Controller response to temperature changes

The effects of controller tuning were examined by varying proportional gain (P) and integral time (T<sub>i</sub>) in the outer culture controller. Increasing P produces a larger jacket-target adjustment for a given temperature error, while decreasing T<sub>i</sub> strengthens integral action on persistent error. In the explorer, each slider ranges from 0.25× to 3× the reference tuning for the selected volume. The inner jacket controller remains at its reference tuning.

To compare warm-up and cooling responses, cell-free medium was heated from 4 to 36.5 °C and held before the target decreased to 33 °C at 16 hours. Each simulation covered 36 hours. Heating-only and heating–cooling operation were compared at 400, 1,000, and 2,000 L, with overshoot and time outside the ±0.2 °C band used to assess the response.

At 2,000 L and the reference tuning, the heater operated at its 20 kW limit and the supply water at its 40 °C cap for most of warm-up. Equipment capacity therefore limited the response, with culture temperature settling within the band after about 7 hours. Both control modes gave the same response until the target decreased. Active cooling then returned the culture to within ±0.2 °C of 33 °C in about 3 hours, compared with about 15 hours under heating-only operation. The slower response resulted from heat removal being limited to passive loss to the room. The biological comparisons below use a constant temperature target.

Metabolic heat was calculated using an assumed rate of 20 pW per viable cell, informed by direct CHO320 calorimetry reported by [Guan and Kemp (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008038515285). The same rate was applied to both profiles; it was not measured for either source culture.

## Fed-batch: heating demand decreases with cell growth

Both control modes maintained 36.5 °C throughout the 14-day fed-batch simulation at 20 pW per cell. Culture-temperature deviations were not visible at the scale of the ±0.2 °C band, although jacket temperature changed with cell density. As metabolic heat approached its 716 W peak, heater power decreased and jacket water stabilized about 0.6 °C below the culture. This temperature difference allowed heat to pass from the culture through the wall and jacket to the room. The peak load was less than 1% below the approximately 722 W heater-off limit, leaving almost no heating demand at peak density. As a result, active cooling was not required and the two control modes produced overlapping responses.

Heating-only operation was sufficient for this fed-batch profile, but peak metabolic heat was close to the passive heat-loss limit. A modest increase in per-cell heat, room temperature, or peak cell density would create a cooling requirement. The result therefore applies to the specified thermal conditions and cannot be generalized to fed-batch cultures as a whole.

## Perfusion: cooling is required

The perfusion profile exceeded passive heat-loss capacity around day 6, at approximately 18 million cells/mL. At the same assumed 20 pW per cell, metabolic heat exceeded the approximately 0.72 kW heater-off limit and peaked above 4 kW. Reducing heater power to zero was therefore insufficient to maintain the constant 36.5 °C setpoint. In heating-only operation, culture temperature departed from the setpoint shortly after day 6 and reached the 40 °C simulation stop on day 7.7. The simulation was stopped because the source VCD history, measured at 36.5 °C, does not describe cellular responses to overheating. With active cooling, culture temperature remained within the band for the full 28-day observation window, while jacket water fell to about 33 °C at peak load.

## Bench-scale thermal response

To examine the effect of vessel scale, the same 14-day fed-batch and 28-day perfusion histories were applied at fixed culture volumes of 2 L and 200 mL. Both cases assumed 20 pW per viable cell and a constant 36.5 °C target. Each culture started at controlled equilibrium, with metabolic heat as the only changing load, as in the 2,000 L comparison. Feed additions are examined separately in the [Feeding](#feeding-temperature-drop-and-recovery) section.

<span id="how-the-bench-controllers-work" aria-hidden="true"></span>

### Bench vessel control

Each bench model uses a single controller responding to culture temperature. The 3 L glass vessel receives heat through an external blanket; the ambr 250 receives heat through a holder block contacting its tapered polycarbonate vessel. The controller regulates element power directly, without a second loop for jacket-water temperature. The element and exposed vessel surfaces, including the base of the 3 L vessel, exchange heat with the room.

### Fed-batch at bench scale

Neither bench culture required active cooling during the 14-day fed-batch simulation. Peak metabolic heat was about 0.716 W at 2 L and 0.0716 W at 200 mL, with maximum modeled culture-temperature errors below 0.001 °C. As metabolic heat increased, the controller reduced heating power and the blanket or block temperature decreased. Heating power remained positive because passive heat-loss capacity substantially exceeded the metabolic load at both bench scales. In contrast, the same profile at 2,000 L approached the passive heat-loss limit.

### Perfusion at bench scale

The high-density perfusion profile also remained within the hold band at both bench scales, with maximum modeled culture-temperature errors below 0.01 °C. Peak metabolic heat reached 4.08 W at 2 L and 0.408 W at 200 mL. Both controllers supplied heat throughout the run, and neither required active cooling under these assumptions. At 2,000 L, the same cell density and per-cell heat produced 4.08 kW, exceeding passive heat-loss capacity at 36.5 °C by more than fivefold.

## Feeding: temperature drop and recovery

Cold feed additions increase culture volume while transiently reducing medium temperature. The day-6 bolus in the 14-day NISTCHO fed-batch simulation was used to compare this response across the ambr 250, 3 L glass vessel, and XDR-2000. Blue and orange curves represent feed temperatures of 4 °C and 20 °C, respectively.

At the same feed-to-culture volume ratio, the initial temperature drop was independent of vessel scale: about 0.89 °C for 4 °C feed and 0.45 °C for 20 °C feed. However, recovery differed between the bench vessels and the XDR-2000. Both bench vessels returned to the ±0.2 °C target band in about 15 minutes with 4 °C feed and 10 minutes with 20 °C feed. The corresponding recovery times in the XDR-2000 were approximately 35 and 20 minutes. Warmer feed therefore reduced both the initial temperature drop and the time outside the band under the simulated conditions.

## Cooling requirements during scale-up

The difference in cooling requirements reflects the balance between metabolic heat generation and passive heat loss at each scale. Both bench vessels maintained the target without active cooling, whereas the 2,000 L fed-batch load approached the passive heat-loss limit and perfusion exceeded it. This difference is consistent with the greater surface-area-to-volume ratio of smaller vessels. At the same cell density, metabolic heat increases in proportion to culture volume, while surface area increases more slowly. Consequently, passive heat loss per liter decreases with scale.

The prescribed cell-density histories generate peak loads of 0.358 W/L for fed-batch and 2.04 W/L for perfusion. Both are below the bench vessels' passive heat-loss capacities, allowing temperature control through reduced heater power. At 2,000 L, the fed-batch load approaches this capacity, whereas perfusion generates excess heat that requires active cooling.

The vessels also differ in geometry, material, and heat-transfer path. Their calculated heat-loss capacities reflect these combined effects and cannot be attributed to surface area alone.

<details class="xdr-details">
  <summary>Geometry and heat-transfer relationships</summary>

For geometrically similar vessels, area scales with length squared and volume with length cubed: $A/V \propto V^{-1/3}$. A tenfold increase in volume reduces surface area per liter to about 46% of its original value. At the same cell density and per-cell metabolism, total heat generation increases directly with volume.

Heat removal also depends on the heat-transfer coefficient and the temperature difference to the room, as expressed by $\dot Q = UA\Delta T$. Wall, contact, and fluid-film resistances contribute to the overall conductance. The table's heat-loss values are calculated from the full thermal network at steady state. [Heat-transfer basis](https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node123.html).

</details>

Perfusion required cooling at 2,000 L even though both bench vessels maintained the target without it. The selected 2,000 L fed-batch case remained within heating-only capacity, but a warmer room, higher cell density, or greater per-cell heat could exceed that capacity. Cooling requirements should therefore be evaluated at the intended scale against the expected thermal load and required temperature shifts, using calibrated heat-transfer and biological inputs.

## Calibration and experimental validation

Vessel-specific calibration and independent measurements are needed to evaluate the thermal predictions. The proposed study would use cell-free heating and cooling experiments to estimate uncertain parameters, followed by separate runs to test predictions. Heating and cooling tests across working volumes, with repeated reference runs, have precedent in [Cytiva's XDR characterization study](https://cdn.cytivalifesciences.com/api/public/content/digi-23201-pdf). The experiment matrix below applies this approach to the XDR model.

### Measurements

Liquid temperatures would be recorded at multiple positions to test whether a single bulk temperature adequately represents the culture medium. These measurements would be synchronized with jacket inlet and outlet temperatures, jacket flow, accessible wall temperatures, headspace temperature, and room temperature. Fill volume, agitation, and bag installation would also be documented. Sensor offsets and response times would be checked before testing.

Measured jacket conditions would provide boundary inputs for the thermal model. Applying measured inlet histories remains a planned extension of the constant-inlet warm-up model. The controller explorer currently calculates inlet conditions from the modeled TCU and circulation loop.

Commissioning would begin with water or a defined aqueous surrogate. Subsequent experiments would evaluate predictions using representative medium with measured or justified thermal properties, since water-based calibration alone would not establish accuracy for every medium. The proposed temperatures and flow changes would be finalized according to the installed temperature-control unit's capability.

The same calibration and validation approach would apply to the bench vessels, including blanket or block temperatures, vessel contact, and exposure to the room. These measurements would test the heat-loss assumptions used in the scale comparison.

## Coupling temperature and culture dynamics

The current simulations impose cell-density histories independently of the predicted temperature. Consequently, they cannot predict how temperature changes affect the culture and, in turn, its metabolic heat. Coupling these responses would require temperature-dependent cellular kinetics together with measured equipment behavior.

### Measured equipment response

Measurements of TCU capacity, sensor response, controller tuning, bag or block contact, and room heat losses would constrain the equipment model. Heating, cooling, and temperature-shift experiments across working volumes would test the predicted thermal capacity and time required to reach a new target.

### Temperature-dependent cell behavior

Metabolic heat could be estimated from oxygen uptake rate (OUR), initially using roughly 450–500 kJ of heat released per mol O₂ consumed as an aerobic approximation. This estimate would require evaluation against cell-specific metabolism, including heat production not captured by oxygen uptake alone. [Gnaiger and Kemp (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-2728(90)90164-Y) discuss the relationship between heat production, oxygen consumption, and aerobic and anaerobic contributions in cultured mammalian cells.

Coupling metabolic heat to temperature-dependent growth, death, and productivity kinetics would allow temperature shifts to affect cellular behavior. A shift from 36.5 to 33 °C, for example, could then change both heat transfer and culture kinetics. In the current results, cell density is prescribed and does not respond to the simulated temperature.

### Sampling, bleeding, and process streams

The feed simulations assume instantaneous bolus addition and complete mixing. Sampling, bleeding, feeds delivered over a finite time, and perfusion inlet and harvest streams would require additional volume and sensible-heat terms in the culture energy balance.

### Gas transfer and other heat sources

Temperature-dependent O₂ and CO₂ solubility, the volumetric mass-transfer coefficient ($k_La$), and CO₂/bicarbonate equilibria would be needed to connect the thermal model with gas transfer and pH. A pCO₂ model would form part of this extension, while agitation heat and evaporative cooling would add terms to the energy balance. The resulting model could be used to examine how a temperature change affects both gas transfer and the thermal load generated by the culture.
